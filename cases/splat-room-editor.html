I furnished a Gaussian Splatting room, in the browser

I rendered a scanned living room as Gaussian splats and built an editor around it — I drag furniture from a catalog, snap it to the floor, then move, rotate and scale it with Unity-style gizmos. This is the story of how I got there, step by step.

Role Design Engineer, Independent Showcase
Timeline October 2026
Key Systems Three.js, Spark, TypeScript, Vite
Use Case Home Interior Staging

Why I Started: My Viewer Wasn't a World

I already had a 3D portrait viewer on my portfolio — React Three Fiber, custom 3D typography, spring physics, the works. I was proud of it. Then I read the roles I actually wanted, and they kept asking for something I didn't have: a 3D editing canvas inside whole generated worlds, with camera behaviour, selection, and manipulation patterns nobody has standardized yet.

That stung a little, because it was true. I could render an object. I couldn't hand someone a world and let them change it. So I gave myself a brief: close exactly that gap, in the open, one thin slice at a time.

  • I read before I built: I installed @sparkjsdev/spark, a Three.js-based Gaussian Splatting renderer, and mirrored all 25 official documentation pages locally. I wanted the renderer's own vocabulary in my head before I wrote a line of scene code.
  • I picked one real asset: a vintage living room scan (a single-file .sog, 1.57M splats) and three furniture models with thumbnails.
  • I chose a use case with a real user: home design. Years earlier I had watched salon customers struggle to picture a haircut from 2D catalog photos, and I built them a 3D preview tool. This felt like the same consultation loop, rebuilt for the browser: walk through a scanned room, stage furniture in it, decide with confidence.
  • I built in slices I could test on my phone: viewer first, then the navigation boundary, then performance, then drag-and-drop, then gizmo editing. Every slice had to run on device before I moved on.

Step 1: My First Room, Floating in Black

I followed Spark's quick-start recipe the way the docs wrote it: one SparkRenderer added to my scene, one SplatMesh pointed at the .sog URL — which the loader auto-detected from the zip contents, no file-type hint needed — and Spark's own SparkControls for WASD and touch movement.

I didn't trust everything I read, though. The docs' coordinate re-orientation quaternion looked like magic, so I pulled up Spark's official SOG example on GitHub and confirmed the same convention there before keeping it. That habit — verifying against upstream source instead of cargo-culting a snippet — saved me more than once on this project.

Then I made a decision about feeling, not technology. I put the viewer on its own fullscreen page — no header, no footer, no frames. Just a loading veil with byte progress, an error card with a retry button, and one small exit button. I believe spatial presence dies the moment site chrome intrudes, and I wanted stepping into this room to feel like arriving somewhere.

Step 2: I Kept Flying Through Walls, So I Built a Fence

The moment I could move, I did what every user does: I flew straight through a wall into black nothing. It felt broken, because it was. A scanned room you can fall out of isn't a world — it's a diorama with no glass.

I refused to hardcode room dimensions; I wanted the fence to fit any scan I load next. So at runtime I read the loaded splats' own extents through SplatMesh.getBoundingBox() — the same API Spark's editor example uses — transformed them into world space, and clamped the camera every frame after the controls update. I opened the floor plan to an 8 by 8 meter roam area but kept the floor and ceiling fenced, and I start every visit inside the room at standing eye height.

Because my clamp runs after the controls and owns the final say, it catches everything uniformly: WASD, mouse drag, scroll wheel, touch inertia. I tested each one by trying to escape. I couldn't.

Step 3: My Phone Told Me the Truth About Performance

On my desktop the room sang. On my phone it groaned. So I counted first: 1.57M splats, sitting right at the Android budget ceiling of 1–2M in Spark's own performance guide. The numbers told me optimization wasn't polish here — it was the difference between a showcase and a slideshow.

  • I cut pixels before polygons: mobile renders at 1x pixel ratio, desktop up to 2x. On a 3x-DPR phone that erases roughly 9x of fill cost in one line.
  • I tightened the Gaussian falloff from sqrt(8) to sqrt(5) — the docs call it perceptually near-identical, and my eyes agreed, while transparent overdraw dropped hard.
  • I switched on Level-of-Detail: a background worker builds a downsampled tree and auto-fits each platform's splat budget.
  • I stopped asserting and started measuring: a ?fps=1 flag reveals a corner meter, so every claim above is a number I read on my device, not a hope I shipped.

Step 4: Stocking the Shelves, Then Throwing Things on the Floor

With the room solid, I turned to the reason it exists: furniture. I built a single-column catalog rail docked to the right edge, rendered from a manifest array — adding a fourth piece means adding one row, not redesigning a page. It felt good to make the catalog data-driven from day one; I've been burned by hardcoded UI before.

The models fought me quietly. Their GLB files carry no unit metadata, so I normalize each one at load to a real-world height — the divan becomes 0.8 m, the armchair 0.95 m, the wardrobe 2.0 m — and I convert every material to unlit shading, because my scene has no lights and I chose visible-over-physical: the wood grain and fabric prints show exactly as authored.

For the drag itself I skipped HTML5 drag-and-drop entirely — it was never built for this — and wrote pointer-based dragging that unifies mouse and touch. While I drag, a footprint circle tracks my pointer across the floor, auto-sized to each piece's extent, positioned by raycasting onto the floor plane and clamped inside the room bounds. When I release over the canvas, the real mesh commits where the circle stood. When I release over the panel, nothing happens. I deliberately killed an early floating-thumbnail idea along the way: all feedback belongs in the scene, not under my cursor.

Step 5: Teaching It Unity's Vocabulary

Dropping furniture wasn't enough — I wanted to arrange it. So I gave the editor the interaction language every 3D tool shares: click a piece and a white ring marks it as selected with a gizmo attached; Move, Rotate, Scale switch from a bottom pill or the W/E/R keys I know from Unity. Translate hides its vertical handle so my pieces stay grounded; scale clamps to 0.3–3x so nothing vanishes or swallows the room; every edit re-clamps into the boundary.

The genuinely tricky part was arbitration. Spark's camera controls and the transform gizmo both listen to the same canvas, and the first time I dragged a rotation ring, the whole room swung with it — nauseating. I fixed it by gating camera updates while a gizmo drag owns the pointer, and by teaching click-selection to ignore presses that start on a gizmo handle. Two small rules, and the two control systems stopped fighting forever.

Step 6: The Floor Was Lying to Me

Then came my favorite bug. My furniture kept sinking — the floor boundary sat visibly under the actual floor. I assumed the bounding-box bottom was the floor. It isn't. The lowest splat centers live well below the surface you see, down in outlier territory.

So I did something slightly obsessive: I decoded the .sog position textures offline and histogrammed all 1.57 million heights. The true floor announced itself as a ridge of half a million splats, about 0.6 m above where I'd put it. I felt very clever — until my runtime auto-scan of the same data misfired on device and put the floor somewhere absurd, probably reading a different splat subset under Level-of-Detail.

I deleted the clever code. In its place: the measured default, plus live [ and ] keys that nudge the floor in 2 cm steps with the value printed to console. One honest number, confirmed by my own eyes, beats an elegant estimate that is wrong. That's the line I'd put on a slide.

What I Learned

Scanned geometry lies about its own surfaces. Any editor that furnishes gaussian scenes needs a floor-calibration story — measured from data, confirmed on device, tunable per asset. I won't ship a scanned-space editor without one again.

Where I Go Next

I'm not done — I see this as the foundation of the 3D editing canvas those roles describe. Delete and duplicate slot straight into my selection model. Multi-room scans want per-room volumes instead of one box. And one day I'll raycast the splat volume itself for true surface placement instead of my floor-plane approximation. But the loop already works end to end: I walk into a captured room, and I make it mine. That feeling — the first time I spun a wardrobe to face the room and shoved it against a wall that stopped it cold — is why I build in slices. I got to feel it in week one, not month three.
